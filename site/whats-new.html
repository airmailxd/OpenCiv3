A few words that come up a lot: playing without Civ3 means playing on a computer that doesn't have the original Civilization III installed, using the game's own art and rules instead. The Graphics Overhaul is the game's own remade, sharper art for the map, which you can switch on or off in Settings. Computer players are the civilizations the game plays itself (the "AI").

Version 0.10.1 (October 29, 2026)

Multiplayer:

  • A new Multiplayer Setup screen, laid out like Civ3's: the game's settings on the left, a slot for each player (Open, Closed or Computer, each with a civilization), the rules, and a messages panel. Choose a scenario there too: Eternal War, Rising Tide, Kings & Nomads or MDJ.
  • Multiplayer games start with Culture Flipping and Scientific Leaders on, and Accelerated Production and No Corruption Near Capital off.
  • Join LAN Game is now a game browser: tabs for LAN, Tailscale, Favorites and History, and columns for players, game type, map size, turn, latency and version. Filter, sort, star your favorites, and double-click to join.
  • Games hosted on your Tailscale network are found without typing an address, and so are hosts you joined before.
  • Fixed: a player who joined someone else's game could lose the map's hills, forests, rivers, borders and resources.
  • Fixed: the host no longer suggests an address no one can join by when Tailscale is off.
  • Eternal War: while others place their mercenaries, a banner says who the game is waiting for.

Rules, checked against Civ3:

  • Accelerated Production halves the food a city needs to grow and the shields everything costs, as in Civ3, instead of doubling what cities make. Cities no longer grow faster than they should, and gold and science are no longer doubled.
  • The Chasqui Scout and the Keshik cross hills and mountains at the cost of flat land.
  • Advances with no era (MDJ's clans' own) can't be researched, as in Civ3.

Scenarios:

  • Kings & Nomads: captured cities can build Settlers again after a game is saved and loaded.
  • MDJ: its own bonuses for irrigated desert (+2 food) and mined mountains (+3 shields), and its own requirements for roads, fortresses and other worker jobs.
  • Rising Tide under MDJ's rules no longer gets stuck between turns. Civilizations know every advance except the one that keeps railroads and a new Palace out of MDJ.
  • Eternal War: the Offshore Platform comes with Writing and costs what a Harbor does.

Other changes:

  • Random can be chosen as your own civilization.
  • City names and production are drawn above units, on a more solid background.
  • Dropdowns and dialogs that were dark and hard to read are now on parchment, and long tooltips wrap.
  • A long message no longer overlaps the answers in the advisor's popups.
  • The advisors' sidebar faces now come with the game when playing without Civ3.

Version 0.10.0 (October 28, 2026)

Maps made the way Civilization III makes them. The map generator is now a copy of Civ3's own: with the same settings and seed, you get the same map Civ3 would make, tile for tile, down to the rivers, resources, goody huts and where each civilization starts. It was checked against more than 8,000 maps made by Civ3 itself, at every world size, landform, water level, climate, temperature and age.

New: Medieval Japan (MDJ), the multiplayer tournament scenario from Civ3: Conquests:

  • MDJ is a game type of its own, with its rules, its clans and its world sizes (1 vs 1 to 8 players). Its maps are Civ3's, too.
  • It plays without Civ3 installed: every MDJ unit is drawn new, from samurai and warrior monks to the Fire Cannon, along with the clans' banners, Jade and Sake, and Civilopedia entries for all of it.
  • Advances are researched in MDJ's own tech tree, which the Science Advisor lays out with its own arrows. Feudalism is the only government, and a resource stays yours once you have had it.
  • Rising Tide can be played under MDJ's rules, with every advance known from the start.

Changes to Rising Tide:

  • The best score wins, and the turns you survive count most.
  • The waves wash away barbarian camps and goody huts.
  • Computer players move inland a ring at a time, build Settlers before buildings that won't last, and go to war for the land farthest from the sea when shut out of it.

Other changes:

  • Dropdown menus and tooltips are now on parchment with dark text, easier to read.
  • Computer players with nothing left to research spend on luxuries instead of science.

Version 0.9.9 (October 26, 2026)

Changes to Kings & Nomads:

  • The city-states are now a player of their own rather than barbarians, so nobody gets a bonus for fighting barbarians against them. Older games are converted when loaded.
  • City-states grow without limit, and their citizens are always happy. Their own tile makes 5 food more, and 2 more for each citizen. They work only the eight tiles around them; citizens beyond those become specialists, each making the city-state 4 shields and bringing every civilization trading with it 2 gold and 2 beakers a turn.
  • City-states build their own armies (two defenders, an attacker, then a bombard unit, up to six) from the best units the most advanced civilization knows, and strike enemies next to them.
  • Your first city-state comes easily: your units fight city-states at double strength until you take one, half again for your second, 0.9 for your third and half strength after. A city-state you take drops to size 5 and needs an Aqueduct to grow past 6.
  • A fallen civilization's cities become city-states only where their land wouldn't touch another city-state's; the rest are disbanded. A city-state's land is its nine tiles.
  • A new City-States panel shows your strength against them and your trade routes with each, with what each brings.
  • Computer players wage wars for good reasons: to knock down the leading civilization, cut a rival's trade with city-states, take resources they lack, or win back cities they lost. They fight one war at a time, make peace once a war has gone their way, and keep a peace for at least 5 turns.
  • Computer players spare city-states they trade with when no rival is near, lead with their King when it's safe, and keep a lighter guard in their first city.

Changes to Rising Tide:

  • A civilization needs a city to live; Settlers alone are no longer enough.
  • A city's own tile makes 4 food, not 6.
  • Flooded tiles are now drawn as coast.

Computer players:

  • Hunt barbarians near their land instead of waiting for them.
  • Count river crossings when weighing an attack, and step round a river rather than attack across it.
  • Train their armies in their most productive cities, with Barracks there built between wars, while their other cities grow and build up.
  • Bring siege engines (Catapults and the like) to their wars.
  • Only offer you trades they would accept themselves; when they turn a deal down, they now say so.

Other changes:

  • "Draft Citizen" appears only once you know how to draft.
  • The end-of-game replay is readable without Civ3's art.

Changes to Rising Tide:

  • Everyone starts with a city of size 5 on the shore instead of a Settler. The first wave takes it.
  • A city's own tile makes 6 food, enough for three citizens (agricultural civilizations get one more by fresh water).
  • Accelerated production is on by default.

Other changes:

  • New games start as a free-for-all.

Changes to Kings & Nomads:

Version 0.9.6 (October 23, 2026)

Two new scenarios, chosen as the game type when setting up a new game. Each opens with a page explaining its rules.

  • Rising Tide. On one continent, Tidal Waves turn the shore into sea a ring at a time, ever faster (21 turns, then 13, 8, 5, 5, 3, 3, 2, 2, 1, then every turn), destroying the cities and drowning the units there. Everyone starts near the edge with a settler, three workers and two archers; settlers found cities of size 5, so you can move your people inland ahead of the tide. No ships can sail the raging sea. A box beside the scoreboard counts down to the next wave. The last civilization standing wins.
  • Kings & Nomads. No one starts with a city: you are a King leading a wandering horde. Independent city-states dot the land, their garrisons growing every 10 turns, raising walls on turn 40. Settlers can only be built in conquered cities. A road to a city-state is a trade route, bringing you its resources and 3 gold a turn. Lose your King and your civilization falls, its cities freed. The last King standing wins, or whoever holds half the world's cities. Played on continents or a pangaea, with a quarter fewer civilizations than usual.
  • Computer players know how to play both: hordes that storm city-states and guard their King, escorted workers building roads to trade, and settlers that flee inland from the tide.

Changes to Eternal War:

  • The Mercenary Market replaces the free unit each turn: a few units (3 on a standard map, more on bigger ones) go up for a secret gold auction. You have 15 seconds to bid, and your bids can't add up to more than your treasury. The highest bid wins; tied bidders each get one. Computer players bid for what they need.
  • The list of cities to send a unit to shows each garrison as unit icons, the map moves to show the city chosen, and you can drag and zoom the map while choosing.
  • Computer players no longer get extra starting units.

Other changes:

  • Disease is rarer: an outbreak takes one citizen, sometimes a second the next turn, and the city is then spared for 20 turns.
  • Bombarding a barricade ends the unit's turn, as in Civ3.
  • With animations off, a battle you can see pauses for a moment on its result, so you can see who won.

Version 0.9.2 (October 21, 2026)

  • Teams. On the player setup screen every civilization gets a team, from Team 1 up to the number of civilizations, or tick Free-for-all to play without them. Teammates are allies for good: they start in contact and at peace with a mutual protection pact, can't go to war with each other (even in Eternal War), and win together. Conquest is won when only one team is left. By default the human players are dealt out over two teams (four on a large or huge world), and the computer players after them. Over the network, everyone picks their own team in the lobby.
  • The end of a game is as in Civ3: the announcement, the victory or defeat screen with the leaders' remarks, the replay of the whole game, and the Hall of Fame. Now and then a historian writes one of the Great Books, ranking the civilizations.
  • From Civ3, with its install: the City View, from the city screen's View button; the advisors speak Civ3's own words; the palace you upgrade with your achievements; the wonder and new-era splash screens; in-game music by era and culture; the tutorial tips (Tutorial Mode in Preferences); and Conquests' Campaign Record.
  • Settlers and Workers can join a city, adding their citizens to it.
  • Global warming: world pollution can now change terrain anywhere on the map.
  • Captives that can be sacrificed give a city culture, once their owner knows the advance that allows it.
  • The City Governor can manage production: what to build often, sometimes or never, and whether to build wonders.
  • Scenarios with Civ3's Debug Mode reveal the map and unlock its editing keys in single player.
  • Fixed: a pangaea, such as Eternal War's, could have land running along the map's top or bottom edge.
  • Everyone playing together over the network needs this version.

Version 0.9.0 (October 21, 2026)

A new scenario, Eternal War, from the main menu.

  • The world is one landmass with no islands, though it has lakes and peninsulas. Every civilization starts in the Middle Ages, under Monarchy, with every Ancient Times advance.
  • Each civilization starts with cities scattered at random among its enemies': 2 on a tiny world up to 10 on a huge one, or as many as you choose when setting up the game. Every city starts at size 3 with a Library, a Pikeman and a Worker. Seafaring civilizations get a coastal capital, and expansionist ones a Scout.
  • Everyone is at war with everyone, for good: no peace, no trading, no talks. Nobody can build Settlers, so the only way to grow is to take cities.
  • There are no goody huts, and barbarians come only as Knights. Cities have no corruption or waste, and a quarter fewer citizens are born unhappy.

Version 0.8.9 (October 21, 2026)

  • Mobilization: once you know Nationalism you can put your nation on a war footing from the Domestic Advisor. Cities building fighting units make an extra shield on their working tiles, only military improvements can be started, and culture is halved until you make peace.
  • Workers in your capital can be traded in diplomacy; they arrive as slave workers. Razing a captured city leaves you a slave worker for every citizen over one.
  • Combat: a won fight costs a full move, even along a road or railroad. Air superiority holds until your next turn. Great Wonders can never be bombarded away, and walls from the Great Wall keep land bombardment off a town altogether.
  • Roads don't take units across a river before Engineering, also where the river runs through a corner between two tiles.
  • Obsolete wonders lose their effects but keep their culture and tourists. A rival finishing your wonder costs you the extra shields at once.
  • Diplomacy: declaring war breaks your peace treaty and your word: other civs trust you less in deals afterwards. An embassy closed by war reopens at peace with its report of their capital. In multiplayer games a city that wants to join you does so without asking.
  • Computer players hurry improvements as well as units, mobilize in hard wars, and use their great leaders better: armies when a war needs them, otherwise the build that saves the most work. Barbarians watch their diagonal as well as all around them, and goody huts teach the advances computer players value.
  • Cities that can't grow no longer offer a Settler that would take their last citizens.

Version 0.8.8 (October 20, 2026)

Hundreds of rules were checked against Civilization III itself: its Civilopedia and editor, real Civ3 saves, players' tests and readings of the game's code. Those that differed were fixed. The new Civ3 rules compendium lists each rule with its source.

  • Combat: retreat odds as in Civ3, and units no longer retreat out of a city or off the sea. Walls and Civil Defense don't add up. Armies share their hit points. Interceptions are fought to the death, a nuclear blast no longer kills every unit, and an upgraded elite unit becomes a veteran, fully healed.
  • Bombardment hits what Civ3 hits: artillery goes after the defenders first, planes after aircraft, cruise missiles after ships. Defensive bombard fires once, from the strongest unit.
  • Cities: the Palace costs more the bigger your empire, and the Forbidden Palace needs half your optimal number of cities. Cities in disorder store no food and may riot, losing a building. Resisters don't riot, and making peace ends resistance. A captured city loses its culture buildings. Iron Works needs iron and coal near the city, and a river anywhere near the city is enough for the Hoover Dam.
  • Happiness works as in Civ3: unhappiness from war comes before luxuries, so it takes two happy faces to cheer an unhappy citizen. War weariness rests at peace, and a Police Station counts as in Civ3.
  • Culture flips are rarer and happen mostly near the other civ's capital. A city that wants to join you asks, and you can rebuff it.
  • Fascism uproots citizens when you adopt it, and its mostly foreign cities make no culture. Losing your capital moves the Palace to your best city, not just the nearest one.
  • Science: computer players choose research as Civ3's do. Switching research and switching back in the same turn keeps your beakers. A captured Great Library can carry its new owner past Education. Scientific leaders can start an Age of Science.
  • Diplomacy: war closes embassies and peace reopens them. Computer players never pay more gold a turn than they can afford, charge you more on harder levels, and demand tribute as in Civ3. The deal screen greys out resources there's no trade route for.
  • Computer players start wars by Civ3's own rules, keep bigger garrisons, and change government in a long war. Their new cities never overlap another city's first ring.
  • The map: barbarians watch all around their camps, raid for the treasury's share per city, and appear in numbers that follow the civs and cities in the game. Goody huts follow Civ3's table. Disease and volcanoes follow Civ3, and volcanoes don't erupt in multiplayer games. Workers can plant forests, and forts, airfields and colonies only go where the terrain allows.

Version 0.8.7 (October 17, 2026)

  • Computer players fight their wars instead of waiting: armies that can't gather enough troops in time attack on their own, units no longer sit fortified deep in enemy land, and a war plan is dropped to meet an attack from someone else.
  • Computer players keep their settlers and workers out of harm's way: settlers go around the land of civs they're at war with and wait for enemies nearby to leave unless well guarded, and workers run to a city when enemies come near.

  • Trading: computer players value luxuries for what they do for every city and no longer sell them for a gold a turn, won't sell to a civ they're about to attack, charge more for advances few others know, and offer you a deal at most once every ten turns.
  • Fixed: a city connected to your capital by a road built up to it later counted as cut off from the trade network.

Version 0.7.8 (October 13, 2026)

A big step towards the full Civ3: Conquests rules. Every rule below was checked against Civ3's own Civilopedia and CivFanatics' studies.

Diplomacy and trade

  • Trade gold per turn, luxuries, strategic resources, world and territory maps, and contact with other civilizations.
  • Sign mutual protection pacts, military alliances and trade embargoes. A pact brings your partner into a war when you're attacked.
  • Break a per-turn deal by declaring war and the computer players remember: they won't make such deals with you again.
  • Harbors and airports link trade between cities across water, and trade is cut by enemy territory and blockading ships.
  • Computer players now propose resource deals, pacts, alliances and embargoes themselves, and offer to renew deals that run out.
  • Espionage works as in Civ3: propaganda, stealing the world map and plans, sabotage with a planted spy, with Civ3's costs and odds.

War

  • Aircraft fly: bombing, precision strikes, recon, air superiority, rebasing, airdrops, airlifts and helicopter troop drops. Fighters, SAM batteries and anti-aircraft units shoot back, and cruise missiles are used up.
  • Submarines are invisible except to submarines, destroyers and AEGIS cruisers. Stealth attackers pick their target, and privateers raid without war.
  • Artillery left undefended is captured; walls defend against land attacks and coastal fortresses against ships, and bombard passing ships.
  • Wheeled units need roads through mountains, jungle and marsh; explorers move as if on roads; armies get their Conquests strength bonus.

Version 0.7.0 (October 12, 2026)

The Civilopedia

  • Civ3's Civilopedia is in the game. Click the book at the top left, or press Ctrl+C, to look up any unit, building, wonder, advance, government, terrain, resource, worker action, civilization or game rule. It opens over the map or whatever screen you're on, with Civ3's own menus and an A to Z index.
  • Every page shows what the thing costs and does, what it needs, and what it leads to. Click any link or icon to jump to that page, and use the arrows to go back and forward. The Description button shows its history.
  • Right-click an advance in the science advisor, or a building or production choice in a city, to go straight to its page. The right-click menus of cities and units on the map have a "View Civilopedia entry" item too.
  • Scenarios with their own Civilopedia text use it. In this version, without Civ3 the Civilopedia had plain panels and no pictures; version 0.7.1 gave it its own.

Computer players: diplomacy

  • When they're stronger than you and at peace with you, they may demand tribute: a little gold, or an advance if you have no gold. Paying pleases them. Refusing means war on Regent difficulty and up; on easier levels they may back down, but they'll hold it against you.
  • Their mood toward you, from Gracious to Furious, now shows when you talk to them (it always said "Cautious" before). It's worked out as in Civ3, from how aggressive each leader is, the governments they like and dislike, gifts, tribute, trades, advances traded lately, shared enemies, captured workers and more. United Nations votes use the same moods.
  • A computer player with plenty of open land left (enough to double its cities) doesn't start wars on weaker neighbours, and asks them for tribute instead. In test games this cut the wars each civilization started from about one to about a third.

Diplomacy: right of passage

  • Civilizations can now sign a right of passage, letting each other's units through their land, from the deal screen. It lasts 20 turns. The civilization asking needs Writing and its own embassy with the other. It can't be signed at war or as part of a peace treaty.
  • Roads and railroads in another civilization's land only speed you up if you have a right of passage with them.
  • Declaring war while your units are inside a partner's land under a right of passage counts as a betrayal: others won't sign one with you again, and it counts against you in United Nations votes.
  • Computer players value a right of passage by the size of both empires and whether they need to cross your land to reach an enemy. They refuse betrayers and civilizations at war with their allies. Now and then a friendly one offers one for nothing, and at war they ask civilizations in the way for one, setting up an embassy first if needed and paying what's asked.

Computer players: growing and building

  • They settle more of the map before going to war: settlers are valued more while open land remains. In test games on island maps, each civilization ended up with about 3 more cities.
  • They carry settlers (and their escorts) by ship to new land, or along the coast when that's at least two turns quicker than walking, building a ship if none is free. Their ships never stay in waters they could sink in.
  • They head for Monarchy early instead of leaving it nearly to last, and their small cities, and cities building settlers or workers, work more food tiles. Together with the war changes below, this gave them about 20% more score in test games and some 50 fewer turns stuck in Despotism.
  • They build great wonders in their safest, most productive cities, never in a city threatened by war.
  • They buy the units that matter with gold (defenders for unguarded cities, attackers for a campaign, escorts, settlers and workers), keeping some money back. At war they raise taxes a little to pay for it.
  • At peace, an army that's too big and costing money is slowly trimmed: one out-of-date unit disbanded every 5 turns, weakest first.
  • They never build Wealth or Explorers any more.
  • House rule: computer players now pay no upkeep for their buildings (they do in Civ3). This gave them more units and advances in test games. You're never affected.
  • On Emperor difficulty and above, computer players now get the same number of content citizens as on Regent, as in Civ3. Before, they had one too many unhappy citizens per city on the hardest levels.

Computer players: war

  • At war they pick one enemy city (a big, weakly held, nearby one), gather enough attackers, siege units and a defender in their nearest city, march together at the pace of the slowest, bombard, and attack only when together they expect to take it. If they aren't strong enough they wait, then fall back to heal and come back stronger. Once a city is taken they garrison it and move on to the next.
  • They declare war on a much weaker neighbour only once their army is gathered and ready.
  • Hurt units (below 70% of their hit points) go back to a city to heal instead of fighting on, and leave enemy land to do it, since units don't heal there.
  • Their great leaders form armies, they build armies at war, and a campaign leads its attacks with up to two full, healthy armies.

  • Great scientists: when a civilization is the first to discover an advance, a great scientist may appear in its capital (a 3% chance, or 5% for Scientific civilizations). It can finish anything a city is building, great wonders included, but can't lead an army. A game setting turns them off.
  • Scientific civilizations get a free advance, picked at random from the new era's first advances, as they enter each new era.
  • Goody huts give what they give in Conquests at each difficulty level, with Expansionist civilizations' own odds. A hut can now also hold a map of the area, or nothing at all.
  • You always get to choose your free advance from Philosophy, rather than it going to whatever your research list said.
  • Under Despotism, an Agricultural civilization's city only gets its extra food if it's by fresh water, as in Civ3.

Maps and play

  • Civilizations start further apart, spread over the land instead of crowded into the best of it at the closest allowed distance.
  • The camera stays on a city while you choose what it builds next, instead of jumping to the next unit.

Watch AI Game

  • Watch AI Game now goes through the usual setup screens, so you can pick the map, its size, the rivals, the difficulty and the rules. It can pause every so many turns, up to 2500.
  • A watched game no longer stops for news. At each pause, a panel lists the wars going on, since when, and which started or ended since the last pause. If the civilization you're following is destroyed, the view follows another one still in the game.

Speed

  • Turns go much faster, especially on big maps late in the game: route finding, worker planning, city site choice, explorers and attack planning all do far less work. The map, fog, units and small map are only redrawn when something they show has changed.

  • Online and network games send their updates with less work for the host.

  • Capturing a civilization's last settler, with a worker on the same square, could break the game later.

Behind the scenes

Version 0.6.0 (October 9, 2026)

In this version a great many rules were checked against what Civ3 players have measured and written up on CivFanatics, and changed to match where they didn't.

Winning and the United Nations

  • A game now ends only when someone wins. The turn limit is a new-game option, off by default (scenarios keep their own).
  • The new game screen has a checkbox for each way to win: conquest, domination, space race, diplomatic and cultural, all on by default.
  • Cultural victory: 20,000 culture in one city, or 100,000 across your empire (scaled by map size, from 60,000 on tiny maps to 160,000 on huge ones), and twice as much as the next civilization.
  • United Nations elections work as in Civ3: every 11 turns the founder chooses whether to hold one; there are up to three candidates (the founder, plus civilizations with a quarter of the world's land or people, or failing that the highest scorer); each civilization has one vote. Computer players vote by how they feel about each candidate and never for someone they're at war with, and a computer founder only holds a vote it expects to win.
  • The United Nations can't be built in a game without diplomatic victory.

Culture and conquered cities

  • Cities can flip to a neighbour with much stronger culture, by the formula Civ3 players worked out: more likely with many foreign citizens, the neighbour's land all around, disorder, and a capital far away; less likely with a garrison of fighting units or a celebration. Units in the city are lost and the new owner gets a free defender. A game setting turns flipping off.
  • Conquered cities resist their new owner. Each foreign citizen may resist (more likely the less their people think of your culture). Resisters work no tile and eat nothing, the city can't hurry production, and each turn they are less likely to keep going. A garrison helps put them down. Your last city, and cities handed over in a deal, don't resist.

War and peace

  • War weariness now follows the points Civ3 players measured, kept separately against each enemy: units in enemy land, units lost, improvements pillaged, cities lost and so on. It fades slowly (a twentieth each turn) after peace. Police stations take a quarter off.
  • A civilization that has war declared on it rallies (war happiness) against the attacker, unless it brought the war on itself with nukes, spies or refusing to leave someone's land.
  • Cities can be traded in deals (never your capital or your last city). Cities given away don't resist, and the old owner's units in them go to its nearest city.
  • A computer player that's losing a war offers advances, or a city if it's losing badly, for peace, and asks for peace itself; one that's winning big demands a city.
  • Bombarding works as in Civ3: it hits the units first, then a building or a citizen. Units are only killed by units with "lethal" bombardment; others leave them at one hit point. Cities never drop below size 1. Wonders, and buildings that wonders give a city (like the Great Wall's walls), can't be destroyed. City walls stand up to land bombardment first.
  • Your units get your difficulty level's bonus against barbarians when attacking and defending; computer players always get a 200% bonus against them.
  • Military police make one citizen content each, up to the government's limit.

Barbarians

  • Barbarians entering a city do one of three things: steal an eighth of the treasury, kill a citizen (only in a city of size 2 or more) or destroy the shields stored toward production.
  • Their stronger units (Horsemen) only appear once two civilizations know Horseback Riding.

Cities and money

  • Buying production costs what it does in Civ3: 4 gold for each shield still needed, double if nothing's been built up yet. Wonders can't be bought or hurried with citizens. Hurrying with citizens costs the nearest whole number of citizens, never none.
  • Wonders are no longer half price for civilizations whose traits match them; only improvements are.
  • Disbanding a unit in a city gives back a quarter of its cost.
  • Choose how much corruption there is when setting up a game, with a slider from 0.50 to 1.50 times Civ3's (0.90 to start with).
  • A celebrating city ("We Love the King Day") wastes fewer shields, as in Conquests, instead of half as many.
  • Policemen cut corruption and waste, and civil engineers add shields when the city is building a building or wonder. The Secret Police HQ only counts as a second palace under Communism.
  • A city in disorder produces nothing at all. Under anarchy, specialists still work.
  • Anarchy lasts 2 to 9 turns, by chance and by empire size, or 2 for Religious civilizations, as measured in Conquests.
  • Switching production never loses shields except those beyond the new item's cost, and the production lists now tell you how many that would be, and grey out what can't be chosen, saying why.
  • Shields from chopping forests never go into a wonder: if the nearest city is building one, they go to another of your cities nearby, or are lost. A city given forest shields can't switch to a wonder until it has finished something.
  • Newly built units start with their experience level's hit points.
  • Workers work at their government's rate (and Engineers twice as fast), captured workers at half speed. Electricity lets you irrigate anywhere, and Replaceable Parts doubles worker speed.
  • Turns without any research no longer count toward the longest a discovery can take.
  • Building pollution now counts in games without Civ3.

Maps and starting

  • Every civilization gets a place to start. If a map can't fit them all at the proper distance apart, another one is made (up to 25 tries) instead of crowding civilizations together or leaving some out. Rejected maps can be kept in an "Invalid Maps" folder to look at (a setting turns this off).
  • Archipelago maps are made of planned islands, about one and a half per civilization, so they really are archipelagos (the first try now works almost every time, where it almost never did). Pangaea maps also come out the right shape far more often.
  • Starting gold and era come from the game's rules, as in Civ3.
  • If making a world fails, you're told why instead of the game crashing.

Playing together

  • Spectators (people who watch a game without playing) can watch as one civilization, as all of them, or the whole map, and switch whenever they like. They hear the news of the way they're watching, as a list rather than popups, and can come back after losing their connection. At most 8 can watch.
  • People joining a game are no longer sent the cities they have never seen, so they can't peek at them.
  • Large games work through the online server: big updates are sent in pieces.
  • The host keeps its bans when it hosts online again, and guests trying the old join code are pointed to the new one.
  • Wrong passwords lock out that computer for 30 seconds, doubling each time up to 30 minutes.
  • Players' names are no longer taken from the computer's user name (often someone's real name); set yours in Settings. Names are tidied and kept to 40 letters.
  • Many protections against guests who misbehave or connect without joining, and the join screen greys out games running a different version.
  • Everything needed to resume a game (seats, bans, password and so on) is saved within a second of changing, not just at the start of each turn.
  • Once you've ended your turn you can't act again while the computer players move, and trades are checked again when they're carried out (for example, that the gold is still there).
  • An embassy's view of a foreign capital stays open in network games, the waiting banner reads properly on narrow windows, and the hotseat screen no longer gets stuck when two things ask for it at once.
  • Units can board a ship on a neighbouring square.
  • You can run your own online server; see Play with friends. The setup guides cover free and cheap server providers step by step.

Saving and loading

  • Games are saved in the game's own Saves folder, with buttons to switch to Civ3's, and you're told if saving fails.
  • Saved games remember the game's dice, so loading a game no longer changes how fights and other chances turn out. The difficulty level is also kept properly on loading.
  • Civ3 saved games and scenarios load more faithfully: owners, starting units, victory conditions, turn limits, fortresses, finished games' winners and much more.
  • Civ3 is found on Linux and in extra Steam libraries.
  • A scenario's art is forgotten once you leave it, and zip files are no longer offered as saves.
  • A save containing something the game doesn't know loads with a warning where it can, instead of failing later. A game that fails to load can no longer be played half set up.
  • A broken settings file is kept as C7.ini.bak instead of being silently replaced.

Interface

  • The domestic advisor's sliders jump straight to where you click, and its maintenance column shows each city's real upkeep.
  • The info box shows your real tax, science and luxury rates.
  • Confirmations (disband, replacing an improvement, declaring war by moving) act on the unit you asked about. Keys do nothing else while a right-click menu is open or you're typing. Clicking a city then dragging the map no longer picks the wrong square.

  • The military and domestic advisors' news waits behind other popups instead of being lost.
  • Picking a random civilization when all are taken says so instead of hanging.

Online play

  • Play over the internet with a join code (like KQ7-4MZ), no router setup needed. Games go through a relay: an online server that passes messages between the host and the people joining. The host clicks Host Online in the waiting room and shares the code; others type it under "Join online".
  • Choose which relay to use in Settings, under Online, with a Test button. A join code for another relay includes its name (like KQ7-4MZ@relay.example.org).
  • A server browser lists the games hosts have made public, with sortable columns, filters for full, password-protected or incompatible games, a ping for each, and a tab of games you joined lately.
  • The host can set a password (it never crosses the network), and remove or ban guests and spectators from the waiting room, or by right-clicking them on the scoreboard during the game.
  • Each player is only sent what their civilizations can know (other players' hidden units, cities, money and research stay hidden). This is on by default.
  • Your own simple moves show at once over the internet, instead of waiting for the host to answer.
  • If the host drops off the relay, it gets its join code back and guests reconnect through it; a room waits 10 minutes for its host. Resuming a game hosts it online again with the same code.
  • The host can also keep spectators out altogether.
  • The setup files and a guide for running your own relay were added.

Network games

  • Simultaneous turns: every human moves at the same time, then the computer players. It's on by default and chosen in the waiting room.
  • If you lose the connection, the game keeps trying to get you back in, and your seats are held for you. While someone's away, everyone sees who they're waiting for, and the host can carry on without them.
  • The host's game is saved automatically each turn, and Resume Last LAN Game hosts it again with the same settings and seats. Guests can rejoin the last game they were in from the join screen.
  • Joining no longer freezes the waiting room for 20 seconds on an address that doesn't answer.
  • After the first one, updates sent to players are far smaller: a few hundred bytes to a few KB, where the whole game (86 KB for a new two-player game, 200 KB for sixteen players) used to be sent after every change. Games run smoother on slow connections.
  • Computer players don't propose deals to someone who has lost their connection.
  • The host's waiting room says which of its addresses come from apps like Tailscale, and the download page explains playing over the internet with Tailscale.

Behind the scenes

  • The relay has limits on message sizes, rooms and connections so it can't be overwhelmed, and its own tests that play games through it.
  • Network connections were reworked so they can run over the relay as well as a home network.

Version 0.4.0, and everything before it (October 4 to 7, 2026)

This fork split from the OpenCiv3 project on October 4, 2026. Everything below was built in its first days and came out together as version 0.4.0, the first download.

Playing together on one computer

  • Hotseat games, where several people take turns at one computer: tick "Human" on any rival when setting up a game. A rival left on "Random" gets a civilization nobody else has picked.
  • Each player can be given a name (or is "Player 1", "Player 2" and so on). Between turns the screen goes dark and says whose turn it is ("Name - Civilization"), so the others can look away, and the next player clicks Begin Turn. Any city screen or advisor the last player left open is closed, and each player's view of the map is remembered.
  • News meant for a player who isn't at the screen, like barbarians stealing their gold during the computer's turn, waits until their turn, one popup at a time; so does what their cities finished building. A trade offer from a computer player brings the right person to the screen first.
  • Players at the same computer negotiate with each other directly: the other player sees the deal and accepts or refuses it themselves.
  • A hotseat game saved partway through a round carries on with the first player who hadn't moved yet.

Network games

  • LAN multiplayer (games between computers in the same home): Host LAN Game and Join LAN Game on the main menu. The host runs the game and everyone else joins.
  • People joining choose their own civilizations in the waiting room (ones already taken are greyed out, and Random is allowed). The waiting room has Civ3's parchment look, shows the host's address clearly, and explains how to find it when the game isn't listed.
  • You can watch a game as a spectator instead of taking a seat. The host keeps a clock for each turn and can end turns that run over a time limit.
  • Games with several people show a scoreboard, which can be turned off when setting up.
  • Updates between computers are prepared in the background, so a big map or a slow computer no longer stalls the game; a computer that falls hopelessly behind is dropped. Badly formed messages are refused safely, and the map view is kept between updates instead of being rebuilt each time.
  • The rules are checked by the host, so for example nobody can change government without going through anarchy.

Winning

  • Conquest victory (last civilization standing) and domination victory (66% of the world's land and people), both on in new games, with progress on the victory screen.
  • The space race: the Apollo Program starts it and reveals the map to everyone, spaceship parts go to your ship rather than the city, losing your capital destroys the ship, and finishing it wins. Computer players build Apollo and the parts, and the science advisor reports progress.
  • The United Nations and diplomatic victory: civilizations vote every ten turns between the founder and the most populous other civilization, and you vote through a popup. (In this version votes were weighted by population; version 0.6.0 changed elections to Civ3's rules.)
  • The score average no longer creeps up or down from rounding each turn.

Capturing cities and units

  • Taking a city of size 2 or more now captures it instead of destroying it. It loses a citizen, its stored shields, its palace and small wonders, and each other building has an even chance of being destroyed; the captor takes the city's share of the old owner's treasury. Both sides are told, and the city is redrawn in its new colours.
  • A captured size 1 city survives if its old owner's culture had pushed its borders out, and its borders shrink to fit its new owner's culture.
  • When you capture a city you choose whether to keep or raze it. Computer players keep every city they take.
  • If the capital falls, the palace moves for free to the nearest city, preferring bigger ones, and you're told where. A civilization without a capital gets one in its next new city.
  • Workers and settlers are captured instead of fought: a captured settler becomes two slave workers. Captives can move straight away. Barbarians don't take captives.
  • Once the last real defender on a square falls, the attacker moves in and captures whatever is left, taking the city; capturing doesn't count as an attack.
  • Buildings a wonder gave a city (like the Temple of Artemis's temples) give their culture to whoever owns the city now.

Combat

  • Units attack once per turn, unless they can blitz (Tanks, Modern Armor, Panzers, Cossacks and armies keep attacking while they have moves left).
  • Zone of control: units with the ability (Cavalry, Tanks and the like) get one free round of combat against an enemy moving past them. It can wound but not kill. Land units only watch land units, and ships ships.
  • Only Marines and Berserks can attack the shore straight from a ship.
  • A winning attacker isn't charged twice for moving in. A retreating defender really moves, and never into another fight.
  • Transports sink with everyone aboard; in port, the passengers are unloaded instead.
  • Bombarding hits only buildings built in the city, never wonders, and can't knock down walls a wonder provides.
  • Nuclear weapons: Tactical Nukes and ICBMs, fired with the bombard order once the Manhattan Project exists. Unless a Strategic Missile Defense shoots one down, a nuke destroys every unit around the target, halves the cities there, wrecks some buildings and leaves fallout. Every civilization hit goes to war with the attacker (you're asked about each one first), and every civilization remembers it as an atrocity. Nuclear submarines carry tactical nukes only.

Armies

  • Armies hold several land units. A unit joins one only when you tell it to. In a fight, one member fights at a time, the healthiest first, swapping in a fresh one when it's down to its last hit point. The army dies with its last member.
  • An army moves one square faster than its slowest member, and its members heal when it rests.
  • A city with a Military Academy can build armies. The Military Academy can be built once one of your armies has won a battle, and the Pentagon once you have three armies; the Pentagon lets each army carry one more unit. Each army needs four cities to support it: you're warned, and it waits in the build box until you have them.
  • Units locked in an army take no orders of their own, and armies are drawn with their first member's art.

Units

  • Units heal in their own and neutral land, but not in the land of a civilization they're at war with (unless they know Battlefield Medicine). Barracks fully heal land units, and harbours ships. Units told to hold now heal too.
  • Upgrade a unit in a city with barracks or a harbour, for gold, keeping its experience: use the new Upgrade button or U. Upgrades only follow a unit's own line (so a Warrior can no longer become a Longbowman), and Leonardo's Workshop halves the cost.
  • Pillage with Shift+P: it costs one move and removes every improvement on the square, except that a railroad is pillaged first and leaves its road. Only armies can pillage more than once a turn.
  • Sentry with Y (wakes when any foreign unit comes near) or Shift+Y (only enemies).
  • Newly built units start with full hit points for their experience.
  • Workers can clean up pollution and nuclear fallout, and finish a job at once when they've done enough work (faster workers used to wait until the end of the turn).

Diplomacy and espionage

  • Diplomatic and spy missions from a foreign city's right-click menu: embassies, planting spies, stealing advances, inciting revolt and Civ3's other missions. Caught missions are remembered. Computer players set up embassies, plant spies once they have the Intelligence Agency, and now and then steal advances from enemies.
  • An embassy page (the E button) lists the civilizations you've met and what your embassy saw of each capital (size, production, buildings, garrison, treasury), with the missions you could send. Setting up an embassy opens their capital's city screen, read-only, showing the land around it as it is that turn. Ctrl+Shift+D opens the diplomacy list.
  • Computer players tell civilizations at peace with them to take threatening units out of their land (two or more units right away; a lone land unit is put up with for two or three turns; a lone ship is left alone). Agree and your units are moved out; refuse, or keep coming back, and it's war. You can tell them the same from the talk screen: they leave, unless they're clearly stronger.
  • An Active Deals button on the negotiation screen lists the deals in force with that civilization.
  • Traded luxuries and strategic resources reach every city connected to your capital, for happiness, building and upgrading, so traded Iron and Coal allow railroads. A resource deal ends, and you're told, when the seller runs short or the capitals lose their road connection.
  • Only advances the other side could research can be traded.
  • News of a war only reaches civilizations with an embassy with one side, as in Civ3.
  • Fixes: deals with gold every turn no longer end at once, deals end on time, and civilizations that are wiped out leave the diplomacy list.

Wonders and buildings

  • Great and small wonders now do what they do in Civ3: the happiness wonders (in one city, all cities, a continent, or doubling temples or cathedrals), research and production wonders, Longevity, the Great Library, Theory of Evolution, Smith's Trading Co., tourism, Leonardo's Workshop, the free units of the Statue of Zeus and Knights Templar, and the Great Wall, Great Lighthouse and Magellan's effects on combat and ships.
  • Small wonders and palaces can be built. A new palace moves your capital, Forbidden Palaces can't go in the capital, and required buildings and governments are enforced.
  • Libraries, universities and research labs add 50% to research; marketplaces, banks and stock exchanges add 50% to taxes and luxuries; factories and power plants add their production bonus.
  • Buildings that need another building first (Bank, University and so on) can actually be built now.
  • Great leaders come from elite units' victories and can form an army or hurry production.
  • Buildings' culture doubles once after a thousand years, counted from when each was really built (before, they were all counted as built in year 1).
  • A city building a great wonder keeps it in its list, and moves on in its queue when someone else finishes it first.

Cities and production

  • A production queue: Shift+click in the production menu or right-click menu. When nothing is queued, your city builds the same unit again. A popup says what a city finished, offering to carry on, pick something else or go to it, and discovering an advance asks what to research next.
  • Switching production keeps your shields, as in Civ3; only shields beyond the new item's cost are lost.
  • Production hurried this turn (with gold or a great leader) can't be changed until the turn ends.
  • "We Love the King Day" celebrations: a city big enough, not starving, with no unhappy citizens and more happy than content ones, celebrates, and wastes half as many shields.
  • Golden ages of 20 turns, once for each civilization, started when your great wonders cover all your civilization's strengths or when your unique unit first beats another civilization's unit. During one, squares already making shields or commerce make one more of each.
  • War weariness: under Republic and Feudalism (a little) and Democracy (a lot), citizens grow unhappy during a war, faster for wars you started and as your units die; police stations and Universal Suffrage halve it. (An estimate in this version; version 0.6.0 replaced it with Civ3's measured rules.)
  • A starving size 1 city stays at size 1 instead of disappearing. A city riots only because of its working citizens, not its specialists.
  • Two points of luxury buy one happy face, and one luxury resource makes one citizen happy.
  • Forest-clearing shields go only to the city whose land the forest is in, and never into a wonder.

Money and research

  • Each government caps the tax, science and luxury sliders (stock Civ3 rules allow 100%, so this matters in scenarios and mods).
  • Running out of money no longer moves your sliders behind your back. As in Civ3, units over your support limit are disbanded one at a time, then buildings are sold, most expensive to keep first, until the budget balances; the palace and wonders are never lost. You're told what was lost, in Civ3's words.
  • Research adds up correctly: commerce moved to taxes no longer also counts as science that turn, and an advance already paid for completes even with science at zero.
  • Free advances from Philosophy or Theory of Evolution are saved until you use them, and you choose your research after the Great Library gives you an advance.

Matching Civ3's numbers

  • City yields, corruption and borders were checked against real Civ3 saved games. Corruption and waste were refitted against 24,235 cities: exact matches went from about 63-76% of cities to 83-89%. Computer players always get the full optimal number of cities, Commercial civilizations get a quarter more, and each courthouse or police station counts properly.
  • Commerce shares are rounded, government penalties no longer cancel building bonuses, Agricultural and Commercial civilizations get their city bonuses, city squares get no road commerce, lakes count as fresh water and give more food, fallout yields nothing, and only humans pay the difficulty's cost for advances.
  • Corruption runs at 90% of Civ3's. An optional new-game setting spares the cities nearest the capital (off by default).
  • Borders: a city's first ring always beats another city's later growth, culture only settles claims of the same rank, and older cities win ties. Being connected to the capital for corruption uses the real trade network and the real capital.
  • More of Civ3's rules data is read from its files and from the game's own rules: wonder effects, spaceship parts, the great leader unit, culture levels and more.
  • Scenario civilizations that start with only units stay alive while they have units.

Barbarians

  • Every barbarian camp starts the game guarded by a Warrior.
  • Each camp has its own chance to release a unit, set by the barbarian activity level, and stops once it holds three units.
  • A camp is dispersed when your borders take it in, whether by founding a city, borders growing or a city changing hands, and you're paid for it.

Advisors and screens

  • The cultural, military and trade advisors show Civ3's reports: culture levels and opinions, other civilizations' militaries, and resources by source and trade route. Every advisor has Civ3's row of advisor faces, the domestic advisor sorts by column, and the space race view shows the ship's progress.
  • The trade advisor only lists resources you know about.
  • The city screen shows taxes, science and luxuries as Civ3 does, what the government's square penalty takes, and the garrison. Worked squares show Civ3's small yield icons, with penalized yield struck through. Long item names wrap on the production button.
  • The Wonders view lists each great wonder's owner and who is building it.

The map

  • Roads, mines and other improvements built or pillaged out of your sight show as you last saw them.
  • Other players' cities show only their name and size. Barracks, harbour and airport icons sit beside your cities, as in Civ3.
  • Workers are shown at work (also after loading and in network games), with a badge for jobs their art has no animation for.
  • Flood plains are drawn, and deserts along rivers become flood plains on new maps. A bare map mode hides everything but the terrain.
  • Hit point bars stay readable for large armies, and cities no longer vanish at the edge of small maps that wrap around.
  • The small map is drawn at its real size in Civ3's muted colours, and clicking it no longer throws the view off the map.
  • Map fixes: rivers at the edge of the map, luxuries piled onto squares that already had a resource, and crashes for cities near the top corners.

Interface

  • A Settings page on the main menu with the interface size, the volumes (0 now mutes) and the new Graphics Overhaul: remade art for the whole map (terrain, hills, forests, rivers, roads, irrigation, mines, fortresses, resources, cities and every unit), drawn at twice the detail in a calm, muted take on the originals. It was off by default in this version.
  • Scale the whole interface with Ctrl and +, - or 0; it's remembered. The window starts maximized.
  • Drag a unit to where it should go. J moves the whole stack.
  • New keys: Shift+Enter ends the turn even with units left to move; Ctrl+S and Ctrl+L save and load.
  • Movement animations are off by default; hold Shift to see them.
  • Selecting the next unit goes through the rest of the last unit's stack first, and Wake All selects only the first unit it wakes.
  • Popups wait their turn instead of piling up, so a deal offer is never lost.
  • "Please wait..." shows properly when you end your turn, and movement points show the right fraction.

Computer players

  • They declare war only when they've really decided to, and only on civilizations they've met and who share their continent; deals between them need both sides to agree.
  • Their workers don't all chase the same square, skip squares they can't reach, and put cleaning pollution first.
  • Their units only go for targets, camps and explore spots they can reach, find a way around blockers, and no longer get stuck repeating a move.
  • Their great leaders hurry production in the nearest city, and they upgrade units while keeping some gold back.
  • Their unit choices no longer break down when none of the choices can attack.

Civ3 files

  • Some leader portraits and animations that showed blank or wrong frames now play correctly (such as the tactical nuke attack and several Conquests leaders), and some units no longer play the wrong sound.
  • Two Civ3 scenarios that failed to load now do. Files from before Conquests, and broken files, are refused cleanly instead of crashing the game.
  • Without Civ3, armies are drawn with stand-in art, and units keep the upgrades they have art for.

Speed

  • Much faster everywhere: route finding (which now also finds the truly shortest route for ships), the computer players' turns, map making, saving and loading, reading Civ3's files, and drawing the map, which is only redrawn when something on it changes.

Download page

  • Each version is now published for Windows, Mac and Linux, with a download page linking to the newest.

Behind the scenes

  • Many new tests, including ones that compare the game against real Civ3 saved games, check that faster code gives exactly the same results, and make the network tests reliable.
  • Large parts of the game were rewritten for speed without changing what happens; the default window size became 1440 by 1080.
